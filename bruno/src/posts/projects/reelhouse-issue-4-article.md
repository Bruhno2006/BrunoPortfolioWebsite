As you might've guessed from the title, I love superheroes, and I love superhero movies. Despite being entirely fictional, these stories of superhumans using their gifts to protect those who can't defend themselves has always compelled me. With every re-watch, they still teach me core motivational values (though personally, I'm more entertained by the crazy fighting spectacles).

My love for superheroes eventually led me to learn about cinematic universes. In summary, these are a storytelling medium featuring seperate films and TV shows, which intersect with each other to form one central narrative. 

So when you mix superheroes and cinematic universes, you get franchises like the *DC Extended Universe (DCU)*, *Sony's Spider-Man Universe (SSU)*, and my instant go-to, the *Marvel Cinematic Universe (MCU)*. But what brought the sudden rise in cinematic universe hype? What exactly made superhero cinematic universes stand out amongst other Hollywood films? I boil this down to three factors:

## Firstly, **superheroes** themselves{.red-text}

Superhero stories were (and still are) the most popular of all comic book genres. The concept of "good versus evil", with heroes having to deal with new/recurring villains every week, really appeals to a huge number of comic book readers (like me). With each new run, they offer an escape from real-world problems that I can get excited about. But what exactly makes superheroes special? I believe the answer lies in how, despite gaining their extraordinary powers, they still retain their core humanity.

Don't deny it, we have all fantasised about gaining superpowers - easily solving everyday human problems without breaking a sweat. However, this fantasy does come with its drawbacks. These drawbacks are explored in our most notable superhero stories, with characters coming face-to-face with the consequences that come with their status. The main problem involves trying to seperate their everyday life from their superhero duties: making up a secret superhero identity.

There are thousands of comics that adapted this kind of storyline, featuring characters that are good, evil, and poorly written - most coming from either *Marvel Comics* or *DC Comics*.

![Spider-Man (Peter Parker)](https://encrypted-tbn0.gstatic.com/images?q=tbn:ANd9GcTVAlegF_A228em-FvipbFOepgV-XeqdBlbiCRQ_HR3ZclR8ef-BfB_Mlte&s=10 "Spider-Man (Peter Parker)")

For example, *Spider-Man* (Peter Parker) juggles both his human responsibilities as if he were in a constant circus act. If he focuses only on one aspect of his life, the other bursts into disaster. Despite contemplating hanging up the costume on multiple occasions, he continued being *Spider-Man*. His main motivation being the death of his uncle, and his parting words ***"with great power comes great responsibility"***

![Batman](https://mediaproxy.tvtropes.org/width/1200/https://static.tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pub/images/batman_91.png "Batman")

Even as a billionare (something we can never achieve), I would say that *Batman* (Bruce Wayne) is just as flawed and human as us. After witnessing his parents' murder, we follow the character as he deals with his PTSD and see how it severely impacts him (through violent tendencies, unhappy mood, antisocial behaviour, etc,). He would later have some personal growth and learn/improve on his mistakes, especially by having *Robin* and the *Bat-family* aid him in his efforts. Even with his various gadgets, strategic thinking, versatile fighting style, and hella cash, his stories clearly show that he still suffers with the same issues us normal humans would, and yet, is still determined to not back down against his villain entourage, superpowered or not. And that is what makes him **vengeance, the night, *Batman.***

## Our second factor: **cinematic** adaptation{.red-text}

![Spider-Man (Miles Morales) from Spider-Man Across the Spider-Verse](https://encrypted-tbn0.gstatic.com/images?q=tbn:ANd9GcRx57zpW-rY6FhVLFS9_kYpVo-3K4vS92AHgCxiF4MOqA&s=10 "Spider-Man (Miles Morales) from Spider-Man Across the Spider-Verse")

The idea of seeing your favourite superheroes on the big screen, either in live action or animation, has brought comic books into the mainstream media. The translation of static imagery into moving pictures provides a new visual spectacle for audiences to witness fights, interactions, and the adapted story in real time in a cinema. Most popular superhero films come from either *Marvel Studios* or *DC Studios*.

Even though we all have our preferences, we can appreciate how both live action and animation have their different benefits for adapting source material. With live action adaptations allowing more grounded and realistic take on superheroes and animated adaptations being used to properly showcase fantastical characters and events without looking out of place.

![Guardians of the Galaxy Vol.2](https://encrypted-tbn0.gstatic.com/images?q=tbn:ANd9GcRmsE01Dc_tnbasAJeheTKkpnLaQav6zByYtIsoI5OuAA&s=10 "Guardians of the Galaxy Vol.2")

An example of a good comic book movie adaptation, in my opinion, would be James Gunn's *Guardians of the Galaxy*. Despite the movie differing heavily from its main inspiration (with *Star Lord*'s mannerisms and costume differing from the comics), I was still entertained by the director's witty, humourous writing and likeable, memorable characters (despite their starting as mercenaries and assassins). The movie also contains unique music choices with songs from the 1900s that make you **feel good**{.red-text}, and climatic ensambles for great battle scenes composed by Tyler Bates. Speaking of battle scenes, all of the fight sequences (choreographed by James Young) are appropriately hype-inducing, whether in space or on the ground.

Even with all this juicy content, it still has a light-hearted story about finding a newfound family in unusual places, which I can rewatch multiple times and still enjoy. Sorry for the ramble, I just really like this film.

## Onto factor three: the **interconnected** universe{.red-text}

![The Battle of New York in The Avengers](https://i0.wp.com/realvirtuality.info/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Bildschirmfoto-2018-04-02-um-11.18.12.png?w=660&ssl=1 "The Battle of New York in The Avengers")

The main factor for the success of a cinematic universe, in my opinion, is a rich, expandable main narrative, which spans across multiple movie instalments, and involves numerous storylines, characters, and in-universe events.

Even though (arguably) the *Universal Monsters* franchise (1931 - 1955) is the first cinematic universe (by featuring classic horror characters from separate movies crossing over regularly), the *Marvel Cinematic Universe (MCU)*, led by Kevin Feige, adopted this concept to become the first successful cinematic universe. The *MCU* has 36+ movies and TV series featuring *Marvel Comic* superheroes intertwined into one huge narrative.

*MCU's Infintity Saga* (Phase 1 - 3) have all their movies leading towards the grand finale, *Avengers: Endgame*. In combination with their meticulously detailed plan, a unique storytelling formula, and *Disney*'s later acquisition of *Marvel Entertainment*, the *MCU* soon became a success, grossing over $32.4 billion in revenue.

*Marvel Studio* establishes the interconnective storytelling through a few methods:

- **Cameos and references**: done through conversation, environmental scenery, or literal character appearances in the film

- **Character development across films**: progression of a character/group's growth and motivations as a character across multiple films

- **Post-credit scenes**: heavily used in most superhero films. Used to present a preview/early showing of future characters, storylines, and events for the main narrative

![Heroes from The Justice League](https://cdn-v2.meme.com/memes/justice-league/justice-league-justice-league-team-poster.webp "Heroes from The Justice League")

*DC Studios* would later be inspired by the *MCU*'s quick commercial success, and they'd produce their own cinematic universe franchise featuring their DC characters - called the *DC Extended Universe (DCEU)*. However, in the overall scheme, despite including fan-favourite characters like *Batman*, *Superman*, *Wonder Woman*, and more, their franchise was classified as a financial bust (only grossing more than $7 billion at the global box office). It needed a soft reboot, changing to the *DC Universe (DCU)* in 2024, led by James Gunn and Peter Safran. I believe the sudden reboot stems from the rushed planning, weak writing choices, and filmmakers not retaining creative control due to constant studio interference.

![Ant-Man: Quantumania](https://encrypted-tbn0.gstatic.com/images?q=tbn:ANd9GcQursfS_0UQYDtABoQ9_MT9_TSoKj5yvynoB_rxzT88Pg&s=10 "Ant-Man: Quantumania")

The *MCU* was also heading towards an audience engagement decline during their *Multiverse Saga*. I had also stopped engaging as much with *Marvel* content because of their decision to produce an overabundance of lacklustre content (mainly for the *Disney+* service) that lost the exceptional story writing. These later enteries show a lack of planning, downfall in consistent quality, and a less clear sense of direction. These were initially the *MCU*'s major strengths throughout their *Infinity Saga*, so this sudden shift is dissapointing to a lot of fans.

In summary, I believe the success of **superhero cinematic universe** franchises' stem from an engaging, expandable storyline, which can be interconnected across multiple separate films featuring fan-favourite characters. This form of storytelling allows audiences to indulge in their main narrative while also being able to enjoy the separate movies without requiring knowledge of the wider universe.

The *MCU* and other superhero cinematic universes are what got me into superheroes in the first place. For that reason, even if they have been downhill, I will always appreciate them for what they are - a good pastime away from the crushing reality of life.
